\begin{abstract}
Solar sails in low Earth orbit are normally expected to lose energy to
atmospheric drag. This paper asks whether a passive, spin-stabilised sail can
instead gain orbital energy, and shows that it can --- but only briefly, and
only within a narrow range of orbit geometries. Of \(900\) cases sampled at
\(700\,\mathrm{km}\), about one in ten gained altitude over two weeks, the
best by roughly \(20\,\mathrm{km}\).

Eclipse is the reason. Over each orbit, sunlight pushes the sail in ways that
both add and remove energy. When the Earth's shadow covers the part of the
orbit where the push is unfavourable, the balance over a full orbit turns
positive; remove eclipse from the same case and the orbit decays instead.

The gain does not last. As the orbit stretches, its low point drops deeper
into the atmosphere, drag grows, and within about two and a half months the
orbit falls below where it began.

To show that the effect is physical rather than an artefact of one
implementation, the propagator used here is released openly and cross-checked
against Ansys STK/Astrogator and Orekit, both of which reproduce it. The
choice of atmospheric density model, not the sail physics, dominates the
remaining uncertainty.
\end{abstract}
